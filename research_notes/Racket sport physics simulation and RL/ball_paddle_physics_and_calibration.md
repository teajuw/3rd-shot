# Ball and paddle physics, and calibrating a pickleball simulation to monocular video (as of November 2026)

Conventions used below. "Cited Findings" are things read from a source in this research session. "Inferences" are my own arithmetic or reasoning from those findings. Values that come from tennis or table tennis are tagged **[TT]** (table tennis) or **[TENNIS]** and apply to pickleball only by analogy. Several sources are self-published (Tennis Warehouse University, Pickleball Science blog, manufacturer pages); this is flagged where it matters. No peer-reviewed wind-tunnel measurement of a pickleball was found.

## 1. Pickleball ball aerodynamics (drag, holes, Magnus/lift, speeds, spin, indoor vs outdoor, equipment specs)

### Takeaway
The reader's Cd = 0.33 matches the best available free-flight measurement for an outdoor 40-hole ball (0.33 mean, but with SD 0.083 between shots); indoor 26-hole balls are clearly draggier (about 0.45). The missing term is lift: measured lift coefficients of about 0.17 to 0.24 in magnitude mean spin produces a vertical force of roughly a fifth of the ball's weight at 10 m/s, and the lift behaves unlike a smooth ball (topspin/backspin asymmetry, weak spin dependence for backspin).

### Cited Findings
- Tennis Warehouse University (Crawford Lindsey; self-published, not peer reviewed) fitted drag and lift to 86 free-flight trajectories of indoor and outdoor pickleballs. Launch speeds 5 to 13 m/s (11 to 29 mph). Two synchronised cameras at 300 fps, digitised in Tracker; CD and CL fitted per trajectory by RK4 integration and least squares; mean endpoint error 15 mm. — [TWU Pickleball Aerodynamics](https://twu.tennis-warehouse.com/learning_center/pickleball/pickleball_aerodynamics.php)
- Same study: outdoor (40 small holes) mean CD ≈ 0.33 (SD 0.083, SE 0.014); indoor (26 large holes) mean CD ≈ 0.45 (SD 0.092, SE 0.013). CD roughly constant or rising slightly with speed across the range; no drag crisis (described as supercritical flow). Constants used: ball radius 0.037 m, air density 1.21 kg/m^3. — [TWU Pickleball Aerodynamics](https://twu.tennis-warehouse.com/learning_center/pickleball/pickleball_aerodynamics.php)
- Same study, lift: topspin low-spin baseline CL ≈ -0.24 (downward), backspin baseline CL ≈ +0.17 to 0.2. Backspin CL clusters around a flat value with little dependence on spin number; topspin |CL| rises roughly linearly with spin number S = R*omega/v. The author decomposes measured lift as CL_Magnus + CL_gravity + CL_internal (an effect attributed to air inside/through the perforated ball), i.e. lift is not zero at zero spin and is asymmetric between topspin and backspin. — [TWU Pickleball Aerodynamics](https://twu.tennis-warehouse.com/learning_center/pickleball/pickleball_aerodynamics.php)
- Steyn et al. (arXiv 2501.00163, third-shot-drop modelling): six outdoor-ball trajectories filmed with one camera (a frame every 0.008 s, every 10th frame used, 12 to 20 points per trajectory). Fitted Cd = 0.30 ± 0.02, constant over Re 4.7e4 to 6.1e4. Lift fit CL = 0.195*S, forced through zero, "large scatter"; measured S ranged 0.03 to 0.3. Position error estimated at up to two ball diameters. — [arXiv 2501.00163](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.00163v1)
- Earlier work used Cd = 0.6 taken from wiffle-ball data (Emond, Sun and Swartz 2024), which the Steyn paper says is much too high for a pickleball. A CFD study (Creer 2023) of 26- and 40-hole balls is described as numerically unstable / inconclusive. The wind-tunnel reference most often cited (Rossmann and Rau 2007) is for wiffle balls, where lift direction depended on hole orientation. — [arXiv 2501.00163](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.00163v1); [TWU Pickleball Aerodynamics](https://twu.tennis-warehouse.com/learning_center/pickleball/pickleball_aerodynamics.php)
- Internal inconsistency in Steyn et al.: one figure uses CL = ±0.12 at S = 0.19, which does not match their own fit (0.195 × 0.19 ≈ 0.037); printed air viscosity units are wrong. Treat that paper's lift numbers as rough. — [arXiv 2501.00163](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.00163v1)
- Pickleball Science (blog) simply assumed CL = 0.075 (half a baseball value) and said it needs wind-tunnel verification. — [Pickleball Science topspin aerodynamics](https://pickleballscience.org/pickleball-topspin-aerodynamics/)
- Ball specification (USA Pickleball, as reported by a manufacturer page and by Steyn et al.): diameter 2.87 to 2.97 in (73.0 to 75.5 mm); weight 0.78 to 0.935 oz (22.1 to 26.5 g); 26 to 40 evenly distributed holes; hardness 40 to 50 Shore D; bounce 30 to 34 in from a 78 in drop onto granite. — [RPM Ball Science](https://rpmpb.com/pages/ball-science); [arXiv 2501.00163](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.00163v1)
- Indoor vs outdoor (manufacturer page): indoor typically 26 larger holes, softer (about 40 to 44 Shore D), thinner wall, slightly lower bounce; outdoor typically 40 smaller holes, harder (about 44 to 50 Shore D), thicker wall, higher bounce. Ball stiffness about 39 N/mm (222 lb/in) attributed to TWU testing. Temperature: below 10 °C stiffer and lower bounce; above 30 °C more compliant. — [RPM Ball Science](https://rpmpb.com/pages/ball-science)
- Speeds: third-shot drops 10.9 to 13 m/s at 15.5° to 22.5° down the line, 13.3 to 16 m/s cross-court; serves 18 to 24 m/s (cited within the paper); topspin up to about 15 rev/s (900 rpm) considered possible on drops, one measured shot 5.9 rev/s. — [arXiv 2501.00163](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.00163v1)
- Peak pro serve speeds from a radar contest: Ben Johns 68.35 mph (30.6 m/s), Zoey Wang 59.03 mph (26.4 m/s) at the 2025 JOOLA Legends Tour. These are contest maxima, not match averages. — [search result: pro serve speeds article](https://marlin-cat-5sw2.squarespace.com/news/pro-serve-speeds-clocked-at-2025-joola-legends-tour-ben-johns-and-zoey-wang-lead-the-pack)
- Spin: a pro serve (Rachel Rohrabacher) measured at 2,273 rpm from high-speed video (hit off a tee). — [The Dink](https://thedinkpickleball.com/rachel-rohrabacher-rips-a-2-273-rpm-serve-in-extreme-slow-mo/)
- A 2022 Pickleball Science article quotes 40 mph (17.9 m/s) as a maximum groundstroke speed (from Pickleball Magazine) and computes drag-free serve limits of about 23 to 40 mph; the contest figures above show this is outdated for current paddles. — [Pickleball Science, how fast](https://pickleballscience.org/how-fast-is-a-pickleball)

### Inferences
- With m = 24.3 g (mid-spec), r = 0.037 m (A = 4.30e-3 m^2), rho = 1.21 kg/m^3: outdoor Cd 0.33 gives k = 0.5*rho*Cd*A ≈ 8.6e-4 kg/m, k/m ≈ 0.035 m^-1, terminal speed ≈ 16.7 m/s; indoor Cd 0.45 gives terminal speed ≈ 14.3 m/s. A drive launched above terminal speed decelerates at more than 1 g initially, so drag error dominates on fast shots.
- The spec mass range (22.1 to 26.5 g) is ±9% and the diameter range changes area by about ±3.4%; since only Cd*A/m is observable from a trajectory, the per-ball effective drag parameter can legitimately vary by about ±12% even at fixed Cd. Fit one lumped k/m per match/ball type rather than a universal Cd. Air density (altitude, temperature) adds further variation in the same lumped term.
- Lift magnitude: CL = 0.2 at 10 m/s gives 0.5*rho*CL*A*v^2 ≈ 0.052 N, about 22% of the ball's weight (0.238 N). A no-spin fit will therefore mis-estimate apex height and landing depth on topspin drives/rolls and slices; this is probably the largest structural error in the reader's current flight model.
- Spin numbers in play: 1,200 rpm is a rim speed of 4.65 m/s, so S ≈ 0.31 at 15 m/s; 2,100 rpm gives rim speed 8.1 m/s. These sit at or above the top of the range in which lift was measured (S ≤ 0.3), so lift for heavy spin is extrapolation.
- A practical lift parameterisation consistent with the data: a_lift = (0.5*rho*A/m) * CL * |v|^2 in direction (omega_hat × v_hat), with CL as a fitted function of S that is allowed a non-zero intercept and different values for topspin and backspin (e.g. start at CL ≈ 0.2 magnitude, or CL = 0.195*S as the conservative alternative). The two published forms differ by up to a factor of about 5 at low S, so this should be a calibrated/randomised parameter.
- Shot-to-shot CD scatter (SD ≈ 0.08 to 0.09 around the mean) is much larger than the standard error of the mean; some of it is probably hole orientation and measurement noise. For simulation this argues for per-flight noise on drag (or domain randomisation of about ±25%) rather than a single constant.

### Gaps
- No wind-tunnel drag or lift measurement of a pickleball was found; all values are free-flight fits at 5 to 16 m/s. Nothing measured at drive/serve speeds of 20 to 30 m/s.
- No measured spin-decay rate in flight for a pickleball. (Robot table tennis models assume constant spin in flight [TT].)
- No tour-level distribution of shot speeds and spin rates (Hawk-Eye-style stats) was found; only contest maxima and paddle-review data. A paddle-review site claims median serve about 60 mph and about 2,250 rpm across 500 players, which is implausibly high for a general population and is not used here.
- The current USA Pickleball Equipment Standards Manual was not read directly (ball numbers come via secondary pages); out-of-round tolerance, test temperature and compression limits not confirmed.
- No data on side force / knuckling at low spin due to hole orientation for pickleballs specifically.

## 2. Court bounce (restitution, friction, effect of spin, standard bounce test)

### Takeaway
The reader's restitution of 0.66 is the top of the certification window (0.62 to 0.66), and that window is defined on granite at about 6 m/s impact, not on an acrylic court at match speeds. No measured pickleball-on-court friction coefficient exists in the sources found, so the horizontal part of the bounce must be taken from the tennis/table-tennis slide-or-grip model and fitted from data.

### Cited Findings
- USA Pickleball ball bounce test: drop from 78 in onto granite, rebound must be 30 to 34 in. Expressed as a speed-ratio COR, sqrt(h_out/h_in) = 0.62 to 0.66; energy returned 38.4% to 43.6%. — [RPM Ball Science](https://rpmpb.com/pages/ball-science)
- Some sources quote the height ratio (34/78 ≈ 0.436) as "the COR"; this is the square of the speed-ratio COR, so check the definition before comparing. — [Pickleball Science](https://pickleballscience.org/?p=2166)
- Steyn et al. explicitly do not model the bounce; no restitution or friction values are given. — [arXiv 2501.00163](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.00163v1)
- **[TT]** The standard robot table tennis bounce model (Nakashima et al. 2010) is an impulse model with Coulomb friction: post-bounce velocity and spin are linear maps of pre-bounce velocity and spin, v+ = A v- + B omega-, omega+ = C v- + D omega-, with two regimes (rolling/gripping vs sliding). The regime is chosen by alpha = mu*(1+COR)*|v_n|/v_t, where v_t is the tangential speed at the contact; a 2026 paper uses mu = 0.3 for ball on table and treats alpha < 0.4 as one regime and otherwise the other. — [arXiv 2608.08157](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.08157)
- **[TT]** The same 2026 paper measured table COR as a function of normal impact speed: roughly constant at low speed and decreasing linearly at higher speed, modelled as COR(v_n) = e0 + s_e*(v_n - v_lim)*sigmoid(lambda*(v_n - v_lim)); the figure legend reads approximately e0 ≈ 0.96, v_lim ≈ 4.8 m/s, slope ≈ -0.034 per m/s (legend text is small; treat as approximate). It stresses that a constant COR is a "major heuristic" and that small rebound errors decide outcomes. — [arXiv 2608.08157](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.08157)
- **[TENNIS]** In Rod Cross's bounce work, a ball either slides throughout the bounce (low incidence angles) or grips, after which the friction force reverses; a tennis ball with no spin needs an incidence angle below roughly 15° to 20° to slide throughout. Horizontal behaviour is described with a tangential coefficient e_x defined on the contact-point velocity, alongside the vertical e_y. Sliding friction coefficients of about 0.5 to 0.6 (grass) up to 0.9 (clay, low angles) are reported for tennis balls. — search summaries of [Cross, Physics Today quick study](https://physicstoday.aip.org/quick-study/tennis-physics-anyone); [arXiv physics/0605040](https://arxiv.org/pdf/physics/0605040); [Sheffield Hallam: effect of friction on tennis ball impacts](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/2149)

### Inferences
- The certification drop gives an impact speed of about 6.2 m/s (sqrt(2*g*1.98 m), ignoring drag). If pickleball COR falls with normal impact speed as it does for table tennis and tennis balls, COR on lobs and hard overheads (vertical speeds above about 6 m/s) will be below the certification value, and COR on dinks (1 to 3 m/s vertical) may be above it. Fit COR as a function of normal speed (constant plus slope) rather than a constant.
- Granite is harder than an acrylic-on-asphalt/concrete court, but for a hard court the surface loses little energy; most loss is in the ball. Ball temperature and ball model probably matter more than surface. A range of about 0.60 to 0.70 with a speed-dependent slope is a reasonable prior for identification.
- Minimum upgrade to the bounce model (slide-or-grip impulse model for a thin hollow sphere, moment of inertia I = (2/3) m r^2):
  - Normal: v_n+ = -e_n * v_n-.
  - Contact-point tangential velocity before impact: u = v_t - r*(omega × n).
  - Sliding regime (if mu*(1+e_n)*|v_n-| is too small to stop the slip): tangential impulse J_t = -mu*(1+e_n)*m*|v_n-| * u_hat; then v_t+ = v_t- + J_t/m and omega changes by r*(n × J_t)/I, i.e. delta(r*omega) = 1.5*mu*(1+e_n)*|v_n-| for a thin shell.
  - Grip regime: contact-point velocity is driven to -e_t*u (e_t = 0 means pure rolling at exit; a small positive e_t models tangential elasticity).
  - For a thin shell the slip stops when mu*(1+e_n)*|v_n-| ≥ (2/5)*|u| (factor 1/(1 + m r^2/I) = 2/5). With e_n = 0.64 and mu = 0.5 a no-spin ball grips whenever the incidence angle from the surface is above about atan(0.4/(0.5*1.64)) ≈ 26°; below that it slides throughout.
  - Consequence for a spinless ball that grips: horizontal speed after bounce is 3/5 of the incoming horizontal speed (thin shell, e_t = 0) and the ball leaves with topspin omega = 0.6*v_t/r. The reader's current model presumably keeps horizontal speed unchanged or scaled by a fixed factor; this is where spin enters observably (topspin "kicks" forward, backspin "sits up").
- This means every bounce in the video gives two measurable numbers (vertical ratio and horizontal speed change), and the horizontal change depends on incoming spin: so bounces are the main place spin is observable from monocular footage (see section 5).

### Gaps
- No measured friction coefficient for a plastic pickleball on acrylic hard court was found, and no oblique-bounce study for pickleball. The mu ≈ 0.5 above is a placeholder borrowed from tennis and must be identified from data.
- No measurement of COR versus impact speed or temperature for pickleballs beyond qualitative manufacturer statements.
- Cross's original equations and coefficients were not read first hand in this session (only search summaries); verify against Cross, Am. J. Phys. 70, 1093 (2002) before quoting.

## 3. Ball-paddle impact (COR, dwell, friction/spin, test standards, mass properties, impact models)

### Takeaway
Certification now bounds both power (PBCoR ≤ 0.43, ball fired at 60 mph at a pivot-mounted paddle) and spin (≤ 2,100 rpm in a 40 mph, 30° oblique test), which gives hard priors for a paddle impact model. The frictionless-face assumption is wrong in exactly the regime the spin test probes: the ball does not slip at steep (near-normal) impacts and picks up tangential velocity and spin, so the change in ball velocity is not parallel to the face normal.

### Cited Findings
- PBCoR test (USA Pickleball): balls fired at 60 mph (26.8 m/s) at the paddle face; the paddle is mounted so that it can rotate freely about a pivot 2 in from the butt end of the handle. — [Pickleball Science analysis of PBCoR test](https://pickleballscience.org/?p=5654)
- PBCoR limit: 0.44 from 1 November 2024, reduced to 0.43 from 1 November 2025 (current). — [Pickleball Effect](https://pickleballeffect.com/?p=40403); [USA Pickleball PBCoR announcement](https://usapickleball.org/?p=62320)
- UPA-A (pro tour body) power test differs: ball fired at 50 mph ± 1 mph at several locations on the face. — [The Kitchen: UPA-A lab tour](https://thekitchenpickle.com/blogs/news/united-pickleball-association-of-america-pickleball-paddle-testing-lab-details/)
- Spin rate test (USAP and UPA-A use the same geometry): air cannon fires the ball at 40 mph (17.9 m/s) at a paddle face inclined 30° to the horizontal; outgoing ball rotation is measured. Limit 2,100 rpm; UPA-A has used this limit since 2024 and USA Pickleball's version is reported as effective 1 October 2026. — [Pickleball Science, analysis of USAP spin rate test](https://pickleballscience.org/analysis-of-usap-spin-rate-test/); [The Dink](https://www.thedinkpickleball.com/usa-pickleball-launches-long-awaited-spin-rate-test/); [pickleball.com](https://pickleball.com/news/usaps-new-spin-testing-the-good-and-the-bad)
- Conflict: a manufacturer compliance page citing Equipment Standards Manual Rev 3.0 (January 2025) says USAP controls spin only through surface roughness and that spin rate is UPA-A specific; this predates the 2026 spin test. — [RPM rules and compliance](https://rpmpb.com/pages/rules-and-compliance)
- Surface limits: average roughness no more than 30 micrometres Rz and 40 micrometres Rt (Starrett SR160); gloss no more than 80 GU; combined length plus width ≤ 24 in, length ≤ 24 in (as printed by the source; USAP's own text was not read); no weight limit. USAP also has a kinetic-friction limit, value not found. — [Pickleball Union](https://pickleballunion.com/equipment-testing-standards/); [Pickleball Science](https://pickleballscience.org/analysis-of-usap-spin-rate-test/)
- Grip vs slip in the spin test: a test engineer is quoted (via a John Kew video) saying that at inclination 45° and steeper all paddles produce about the same spin because the ball does not slip; paddle differences appear only below 45° and are largest near 30°. Pickleball Science argues that dwell time, not surface friction/roughness, is the main determinant of spin, and that roughness tests do not predict spin. — [Pickleball Science, analysis of USAP spin rate test](https://pickleballscience.org/analysis-of-usap-spin-rate-test/); [Pickleball Science](https://pickleballscience.org/?p=5827)
- Dwell (contact) time: "on the order of four milliseconds" (an estimate from a physics analysis, not a rig measurement). — [Pickleball Science](https://pickleballscience.org/?p=4210)
- Paddle mass: average static weight about 8 oz (227 g); under 8 oz "light", over "heavy". Swing weight is the moment of inertia about a pivot on the handle (parallel-axis: I_swing = I_cm + m*r^2), quoted in kg·cm^2. — [Pickleball Science](https://pickleballscience.org/?p=4210)
- Twist weight (moment of inertia about the long axis): review-site classification below 6.0 low, 6.0 to 6.8 typical, above 6.8 high (kg·cm^2, measured on a Briffidi SW1 with a 5 cm pickleball pivot adapter). — [Pickleball Effect](https://pickleballeffect.com/?p=40680)
- **[TT]** Racket impact modelling in robot table tennis: the common model is a linear impulse map with constant coefficients (same A, B, C, D structure as the table bounce, applied in the racket frame after subtracting racket velocity). It cannot capture velocity-dependent rubber behaviour; recent fixes are (a) residual neural networks on top of the linear model, (b) coefficient matrices that are linear functions of tangential and normal impact speed, and (c) Gaussian-process models of the impulse-model parameters (normal COR and tangential impulse response) conditioned on incoming velocity and spin, fitted from a dataset of racket bounces over 10 racket configurations (8,194 bounces per search summary), which reduced post-impact velocity and spin error for all racket types versus constant parameters. — [arXiv 2608.08157](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.08157); [arXiv 2604.11349](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.11349)
- **[TT]** Limits of impulse models: with an impulse-based racket contact model, optimised robot serves saturate at about 40 rev/s while skilled humans reach about 60 rev/s, attributed to prolonged brushing contact that an instantaneous impulse cannot represent. — [arXiv 2608.08157](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.08157)
- **[TENNIS]** On strings, a ball slides throughout the impact only at angles under about 40° to the string plane; steeper impacts grip. — search summary of [Cross, oblique impact of a tennis ball on strings](https://www.bisp-surf.de/Record/PU201103001936)

### Inferences
- Recommended paddle impact model (the same slide-or-grip impulse model as the court bounce, applied in the paddle frame): relative velocity w = v_ball - v_paddle(contact point). Normal: w_n+ = -e_A*w_n-. Tangential: contact-point slip u = w_t - r*(omega × n); if mu_p*(1+e_A)*|w_n-| ≥ (2/5)*|u| the ball grips (exit slip = -e_t*u), else it slides with impulse mu_p*(1+e_A)*m*|w_n-|. Parameters: e_A (apparent normal COR, including paddle recoil), mu_p, e_t. The spin-test observation (no slip at ≥ 45°, slip near 30°) puts the slide/grip boundary between those angles, which bounds mu_p: grip at 45° requires mu_p*(1+e_A) ≥ 0.4, slip at 30° (tan of angle from surface = 0.577) requires mu_p*(1+e_A) < 0.4/0.577 ≈ 0.69; with e_A ≈ 0.4 this gives mu_p roughly 0.29 to 0.5.
- Sanity check against the spin cap: in the grip regime a spinless ball hitting at 40 mph (17.9 m/s) at 30° has tangential speed 15.5 m/s; thin-shell grip with e_t = 0 gives exit rim speed 0.6 × 15.5 = 9.3 m/s, i.e. about 2,400 rpm at r = 0.037 m. Sliding at mu_p*(1+e_A) = 0.6 gives rim speed 1.5 × 0.6 × 8.95 = 8.1 m/s, about 2,080 rpm. So the 2,100 rpm cap is close to the theoretical ceiling of this rigid model, and the model is at least consistent in magnitude. (This is my arithmetic, not a published validation.)
- Error in the reader's paddle-normal estimate: with a frictionless face, delta-v is exactly along the normal. With friction the tangential impulse is up to mu_p times the normal impulse while sliding (angle error up to atan(mu_p), about 16° to 27° for mu_p 0.3 to 0.5), and in the grip regime the tangential delta-v is 0.4*u for a thin shell. The error grows with brushing strokes (large tangential paddle velocity) and with incoming spin. The inverse problem is then under-determined from delta-v alone: unknowns are the face normal (2), paddle velocity at contact (3) and incoming/outgoing spin, against 3 observed delta-v components. It needs extra constraints: paddle velocity from pose/wrist tracking, outgoing spin inferred from the following flight and bounce, and priors on swing direction.
- PBCoR 0.43 is measured on a paddle free to pivot, so it is probably closer to an apparent (recoil-inclusive) ratio at 60 mph than a rigid-wall COR, and a hand-held paddle swung by an arm has a larger effective mass. Treat e_A as a fitted parameter with a prior around 0.4 to 0.5 at high relative speed, higher at low speed (dinks), and varying with impact location (lower near the edge/tip of the face; high twist weight reduces the fall-off).
- Mass ratio: ball 24 g vs paddle about 227 g means recoil is not negligible (a free paddle hit at its centre of mass has reduced-mass factor about 0.9), but the hand and arm add effective mass; a single e_A absorbs this.
- Dwell of about 4 ms at relative speed 20 m/s is tens of millimetres of paddle travel during contact, and the face may rotate by a degree or more during contact on a fast swing; an impulse model with an "effective" normal is an approximation for heavily brushed shots (compare the table tennis saturation finding).

### Gaps
- Could not confirm from a primary document exactly what PBCoR is (e.g. ratio of rebound to incident ball speed vs a collision COR corrected for paddle recoil) or the measured distribution across paddles. The USAP SOPs were not read.
- No published pickleball paddle-ball friction coefficient, tangential COR, or dwell-time measurement by high-speed video was found; 4 ms is an estimate from a blog.
- Typical swing weight values (commonly quoted by reviewers as roughly 100 to 130 kg·cm^2) and paddle thickness (commonly 13 to 16 mm) were not confirmed from a source in this session.
- No pickleball-specific sweet-spot COR map was found.

## 4. How racket-sport simulation and robot systems model and calibrate flight, bounce and racket contact

### Takeaway
The dominant recipe in robot table tennis is a small physics core (gravity, quadratic drag, Magnus with spin assumed constant in flight, impulse bounce with Coulomb friction) whose few parameters are identified from tracked trajectories, with learned corrections added only where the physics is known to be weak (racket contact, speed-dependent COR). Contacts are handled as discrete events (reset maps) rather than simulated through time.

### Cited Findings
- **[TT]** Flight ODE used in a 2026 robot serve paper: m*dv/dt = -k_d*|v|*v + k_m*(omega × v) + m*g, with m = 2.7 g, k_d = 3.8e-3 kg/m (as printed), k_m = 3.0e-6 kg; spin assumed constant during flight. — [arXiv 2608.08157](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.08157)
- **[TT]** The same paper identifies table COR experimentally as a function of normal impact speed (plateau then linear decrease, blended by a sigmoid to stay differentiable) rather than assuming a constant. — [arXiv 2608.08157](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.08157)
- **[TT]** Integration: RK4 keeps mean trajectory error sub-centimetre for step sizes up to 0.2 s over 10,000 sampled initial conditions (speeds 2 to 20 m/s, spins 0 to 150 rev/s) versus a high-accuracy reference; explicit Euler is far worse. — [arXiv 2608.08157](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.08157)
- **[TT]** Contact handling: fixed-step time stepping needs very small steps to stop the ball penetrating the table and makes gradients ill-conditioned; smoothing/penalty contact introduces stiffness and approximation error; the authors instead optimise event times (bounce, net crossing, landing) directly and apply the bounce as an explicit reset map between smooth flight phases, with net clearance as a constraint at the net-crossing time. Implemented in JAX with automatic differentiation; 4.1× faster than fixed-step with root finding at similar accuracy. — [arXiv 2608.08157](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.08157)
- **[TT]** Real-world result of that pipeline: mean landing error 13.1 ± 7.3 cm and spin error 4.5 ± 3.8 rev/s on a real robot despite near-zero simulated error; the authors attribute the gap to perception and calibration accuracy and launcher repeatability. — [arXiv 2608.08157](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.08157)
- **[TT]** Racket contact identification: pre- and post-impact velocities are estimated by fitting straight lines to positions in a roughly 50 ms window either side of the bounce (drag and Magnus negligible over that window); impulse-model parameters are then regressed (Gaussian processes conditioned on incoming velocity and spin). — search summary and abstract of [arXiv 2604.11349](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.11349)
- **[TT]** Grey-box filtering: a 2023 paper keeps drag, spin and table impacts as a physics base with some parameters learned from data, inside an extended/unscented Kalman filter for prediction. — search summary of [arXiv 2305.15189](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.15189v2.pdf)
- **[TT]** Spin from the Magnus term: a 2019 robot table tennis paper fits spin from trajectory curvature and notes the result depends on the Magnus coefficient rather than the drag coefficient. — search summary of [arXiv 1905.07967](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.07967)
- **[TT]** Broadcast-video work generates training trajectories in MuJoCo, varying initial position, velocity, spin and camera parameters (140,000 synthetic trajectories). — [arXiv 2511.20250](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.20250v1)
- Pickleball: the only fitted pickleball flight models found (TWU, Steyn et al.) fit CD and CL per trajectory by least squares on tracked positions with RK4/Euler integration; neither models bounce or paddle. — [TWU](https://twu.tennis-warehouse.com/learning_center/pickleball/pickleball_aerodynamics.php); [arXiv 2501.00163](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.00163v1)

### Inferences
- The printed table tennis k_d = 3.8e-3 kg/m looks about ten times larger than 0.5*rho*Cd*A for a 40 mm ball with Cd ≈ 0.4 to 0.5 (about 3e-4 to 4e-4 kg/m); do not reuse it without checking. The structure of the model is the useful part.
- Direct transfer to pickleball: (1) fit the lumped drag k/m and lift function per ball type from many flight segments jointly (shared aerodynamic parameters, per-segment initial state and spin); (2) fit bounce parameters (e_n(v_n), mu, e_t) from the pre/post-bounce line fits across all bounces; (3) fit paddle parameters from pre/post-hit velocities with pose-derived paddle velocity as input; (4) only then add a small residual model on the bounce and hit maps if structured residuals remain.
- Event-based simulation (flight ODE between analytically resolved events: court plane crossing, net plane crossing, paddle contact) is better suited than a general time-stepping contact engine for both fitting and RL: it is exact at the bounce, differentiable, and fast.
- The table tennis line-fit window trick needs adapting: at 30 or 60 fps broadcast there are only 1 to 3 frames within 50 ms of a bounce, so velocities either side should come from the fitted flight segments evaluated at the bounce time, not from local line fits.

### Gaps
- The well-known Google DeepMind robot table tennis papers (system case study, i-Sim2Real, GoalsEye, human-level competitive play) and tennis/badminton robot systems were not read in this session; from background knowledge they use MuJoCo-based ball models with system-identified drag/restitution, added latency/noise modelling and real-data fine-tuning, but specific parameter values and procedures are unverified here.
- The full text of arXiv 2604.11349 (numerical errors of constant vs GP racket models) could not be retrieved (PDF too large).
- No published badminton shuttle or tennis robot calibration details were collected.

## 5. Estimating spin from video when the ball is small and blurred

### Takeaway
With broadcast footage, spin is not seen directly; it is inferred from its effects, namely the Magnus curvature of the flight and the change in velocity at the bounce. Published monocular results validate only the sign of spin (topspin vs backspin, about 90 to 97% accurate in table tennis), not its magnitude.

### Cited Findings
- **[TT]** Kienzle et al. (CVPR Workshops 2025): a network trained only on physically simulated trajectories infers initial spin and the 3D trajectory from the 2D ball track in monocular broadcast video; 92.0% spin classification accuracy and 2D reprojection error 0.19% of the image diagonal on real data. — [arXiv 2504.19863](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.19863)
- **[TT]** That line of work states that the Magnus effect is the main influence of spin during flight and spin-induced friction matters only at the bounce. — search summary of [arXiv 2504.19863](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.19863)
- **[TT]** "Uplifting Table Tennis" (November 2025) adds a ball detector and a 13-keypoint table detector in front of a transformer "uplifting" network with a learnable spin token; time is encoded with real timestamps so it tolerates 20 to 60 fps and missing detections. Spin is evaluated only as binary topspin vs backspin: 89.5% accuracy (F1 0.900) on 57 broadcast trajectories, 97.1% on 34 trajectories from another set. No rev/s error is reported because no continuous spin ground truth exists; 3D accuracy is reported only as reprojection error (ball about 9 to 12 px at 1920×1080). — [arXiv 2511.20250](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.20250v1)
- **[TT]** Failure modes reported: player feet or the paddle detected as the ball for several consecutive frames (not reliably removed by Kalman filtering), occlusion of ball and table keypoints, and strict 2-pixel ball detection accuracy of only 75%. — [arXiv 2511.20250](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.20250v1)
- **[TT]** With dedicated cameras, robot systems measure spin directly from ball markings (e.g. the SpinDOE dot-pattern method), and a robot system using it reports spin error of 4.5 ± 3.8 rev/s between planned and measured serves. — [arXiv 2608.08157](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.08157)
- **[TT]** Trajectory-based spin fitting depends on the assumed Magnus coefficient, not the drag coefficient. — search summary of [arXiv 1905.07967](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.07967)
- Pickleball: even with 300 fps stereo-like capture, per-trajectory lift coefficients show large scatter, and the single-camera six-shot study had position errors up to two ball diameters. — [TWU](https://twu.tennis-warehouse.com/learning_center/pickleball/pickleball_aerodynamics.php); [arXiv 2501.00163](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.00163v1)

### Inferences
- Observability in pickleball is worse than in table tennis for flight (lower spin numbers, short flights of 0.3 to 1.5 s, and an uncertain CL(S) relation), but lift is about 20% of g at 10 m/s, which over a 1 s flight displaces the ball by roughly 1 m relative to a no-spin arc, so the sign and rough size of topspin/backspin should be recoverable on drives, serves and drops. Dinks and fast volley exchanges are too short/slow.
- From a single camera, Magnus-induced vertical curvature is partly confounded with depth error and with drag; sidespin (lateral curve) is confounded with camera-ray ambiguity. Joint fitting across the whole segment with both endpoints anchored (known hit location from player pose, bounce on the court plane) breaks most of this ambiguity.
- Because spin and CL only appear as the product CL(S)*direction, spin magnitude from flight is only as good as the lift model; given the factor-of-several disagreement between published CL forms, estimate an "effective lift acceleration coefficient" per flight and convert to rpm only with stated uncertainty.
- The bounce is the stronger spin cue: with the slide-or-grip model, the horizontal velocity change at the bounce depends on r*omega directly (independent of aerodynamics). Fit flight and bounce jointly: spin at the end of flight segment i must explain both the curvature of segment i and the horizontal speed change into segment i+1. The post-bounce spin is in turn predicted by the model (in the grip regime it is 0.6*v_t/r topspin regardless of incoming spin), which gives a strong prior for the incoming spin of the next hit.
- The table tennis approach of training an uplifting network on simulator output is directly applicable once the pickleball simulator is calibrated; its input robustness tricks (timestamps instead of frame indices, augmentation with missing detections) match the broadcast setting.
- Realistic accuracy expectation: reliable sign classification, magnitude perhaps within several hundred rpm on long flights. This is my estimate; there is no published pickleball benchmark.

### Gaps
- No method or benchmark for spin magnitude from monocular video in pickleball; no ground-truth spin dataset for pickleball broadcast footage.
- No study of using motion-blur streak shape, hole pattern or logo tracking on pickleballs was found (the hole pattern could in principle give direct spin at high resolution/shutter speed).
- The physics coefficients inside the table tennis synthetic-data generators were not available on the pages read.

## 6. Sim-to-real practice when "real" is monocular video

### Takeaway
The setting calls for real-to-sim system identification by reprojection: optimise a small set of physical parameters so that simulated 3D trajectories, projected through the calibrated camera, match the tracked 2D ball, then randomise around the fitted values and add small residual models only where structured error remains. Methods that need interventions on a real system (closed-loop robot rollouts) do not apply; there is only passive footage.

### Cited Findings
- Monocular inverse-physics work (NeuPhysics 2022, MonoPhysics 2026, ReconPhys) estimates physical parameters by coupling a differentiable simulator to video through a rendering or cycle-consistency loss; the stated main difficulty of the monocular setting is missing multi-view constraints, giving scale ambiguity and inaccurate geometry. These papers target deformable objects, not ball sports. — [NeuPhysics, arXiv 2210.12352](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.12352); [MonoPhysics, arXiv 2605.30320](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30320)
- **[TT]** Physically grounded synthetic-to-real transfer: networks trained purely on simulated trajectories generalise to real broadcast video when the simulation is physically correct and the input representation and augmentations are chosen for it (2D track plus table keypoints rather than pixels). — [arXiv 2504.19863](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.19863); [arXiv 2511.20250](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.20250v1)
- **[TT]** Without 3D ground truth, these works evaluate by 2D reprojection error (as a fraction of image diagonal or in pixels). — [arXiv 2511.20250](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.20250v1)
- **[TT]** Residual modelling on a physics base is the established fix for racket contact (residual networks, or GPs over physical parameters to keep interpretability). — [arXiv 2608.08157](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.08157); [arXiv 2604.11349](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.11349)
- **[TT]** Even with identified physics, the residual real-world error was dominated by perception and calibration accuracy rather than by the dynamics model (13 cm landing error vs near-zero in simulation). — [arXiv 2608.08157](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.08157)
- MuJoCo's documentation recommends its direct stiffness/damping contact parameterisation for system identification, and suggests solimp[0] = 0 for smooth, differentiable dynamics. — [MuJoCo modelling docs](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/stable/modeling.html)
- A 2025 paper on differentiable MuJoCo (MJX) notes that penalty contacts give poor gradients unless contacts are softened or timesteps made very small, and proposes adaptive internal timesteps; it includes a racket-ball task. — search summary of [arXiv 2506.14186](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14186v2)

### Inferences
- Suitability ranking for this project:
  1. System identification by trajectory matching (best fit). Global parameters shared across the dataset (k/m per ball type, CL function, e_n(v_n), mu_court, e_t, paddle e_A, mu_p) plus per-segment latent variables (initial state, spin). The loss should be 2D reprojection error in pixels, weighted by detection uncertainty, not error against an already-reconstructed 3D track, because monocular 3D reconstruction already assumed a physics model and would bias the fit toward it.
  2. Differentiable event-based simulation (JAX-style flight ODE plus reset maps) to make (1) a gradient-based joint optimisation; much simpler than differentiating through a contact engine.
  3. Residual physics, after (1): small learned corrections to the bounce map and paddle map as a function of impact state. Risk with monocular data: residuals will absorb camera calibration, rolling-shutter and tracking bias, so check residuals for dependence on image position/depth before attributing them to physics.
  4. Domain randomisation for anything trained in the simulator (RL policies, uplifting networks): randomise over the posterior/spread from (1), e.g. drag ±12 to 25%, CL over the range between the two published forms, COR 0.60 to 0.70 with speed slope, friction ranges, wind outdoors, plus observation noise matching tracker error. This covers parameters that the video cannot pin down.
  5. Robot-style iterative sim-to-real loops (deploy policy, collect real rollouts, refit) are not available; the nearest equivalent is held-out replay: initialise the simulator at a real hit and compare the simulated continuation with the observed one.
- Identifiability checks worth doing: (a) drag vs depth/scale: a mis-scaled court homography looks like a drag error, so fit camera parameters first from court lines and hold them fixed, or include them with tight priors; (b) COR vs frame timing: at 30 fps the bounce instant is uncertain by up to ±17 ms, so solve for bounce time from the intersection of flight fits with the court plane; (c) outdoor wind is a per-rally nuisance parameter with the same signature as a drag/lift change.
- Validation metrics that match the reader's goal: open-loop prediction error over one flight segment given only the previous segment; landing-point error in cm; time-of-flight error; net-clearance error; distribution-level comparisons (rally length, bounce depth histograms) between simulated and reconstructed rallies. Per memory of this project's own practice, any held-out set should be a random sample of rallies, not hand-picked ones.
- Since perception dominated error even in a calibrated stereo robot lab, expect the same here: improving ball detection near bounces/hits and camera calibration may reduce the sim-to-video gap more than adding physics terms beyond lift and a friction bounce.

### Gaps
- No published real-to-sim pipeline fits ball-sport simulator parameters (drag, lift, restitution, friction) to monocular broadcast video with reported parameter accuracy; the nearest works train on a fixed simulator and report 2D reprojection and spin sign only.
- Foundational domain-randomisation and sim-parameter-adaptation papers (e.g. Tobin et al. 2017, SimOpt, TossingBot residual physics) and the DeepMind table tennis sim-to-real papers were not read in this session, so no specifics are quoted from them.

## 7. Representing a light hollow ball, drag/Magnus and fast thin contacts in MuJoCo

### Takeaway
MuJoCo can produce quadratic drag and a Magnus force through its ellipsoid fluid model, but its coefficients are not the sports-aerodynamics CD/CL and it cannot express a spin-dependent CL with an intercept or a topspin/backspin asymmetry, so applying custom aerodynamic forces is the cleaner route. Its soft contacts cannot be made stiff enough for a roughly 4 ms impact unless the timestep is well under 1 ms, so restitution and spin transfer are better imposed by an explicit impulse (event) model.

### Cited Findings
- MuJoCo has two stateless fluid-force models enabled by setting the `density` and `viscosity` options positive; `wind` is a 3D vector subtracted from body velocity. The inertia-box model derives quadratic drag and viscous resistance from each body's mass and inertia (an equivalent box). The ellipsoid model is enabled per geom with `fluidshape="ellipsoid"` and has five `fluidcoef` values: blunt drag (default 0.5), slender drag (0.25), angular drag (1.5), Kutta lift (1.0), Magnus lift (1.0). Its components are added mass (not scalable), viscous drag, angular drag, Magnus lift proportional to density × volume × (omega × v), Kutta lift, and Stokes viscous resistance. — [MuJoCo fluid forces](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/stable/computation/fluid.html)
- The docs state that implicit integration significantly improves stability with velocity-dependent forces and recommend the `implicit` or `implicitfast` integrators when fluid forces are used. — [MuJoCo fluid forces](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/stable/computation/fluid.html)
- Contact softness: the solref time constant should be at least two times the simulation timestep (enforced by the compiler unless `refsafe` is disabled); a larger time constant is softer. In the positive format a damping ratio below 1 gives bouncy contacts. Negative solref values are read as (-stiffness, -damping) directly; zero damping gives perfectly elastic collisions; this format is recommended for system identification. — [MuJoCo modelling docs](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/stable/modeling.html)
- Friction: geoms specify sliding, torsional and rolling friction; `condim` selects which are active; elliptic friction cones are closer to physical reality than the default pyramidal; to suppress slip use elliptic cones, large `impratio`, Newton solver with very small tolerance, then the noslip solver if needed. Contact parameters mix by priority/maximum between the two geoms. — [MuJoCo modelling docs](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/stable/modeling.html)
- A community cheat sheet suggests solref "0.02 0.1" (time constant 0.02 s, damping ratio 0.1) for a bouncy table tennis ball as a starting point only. — [RoboLaWeb solref/solimp cheat sheet](https://robolaweb.gitbook.io/robolaweb-docs/basic-concept/solref-solimp-parameter-cheat-sheet)
- **[TT]** Table tennis researchers avoid fixed-step contact for the ball for the reasons above (tiny steps needed to prevent penetration; ill-conditioned gradients) and use event-time reset maps. MuJoCo has nevertheless been used to generate large synthetic table tennis trajectory sets. — [arXiv 2608.08157](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.08157); [arXiv 2511.20250](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.20250v1)

### Inferences
- Mapping the Magnus coefficient: if sports lift is CL = k*S with S = r*omega/v, then F = 0.5*rho*A*k*r*omega*v = (3k/8)*rho*V*omega*v for a sphere (V = 4/3*pi*r^3). MuJoCo's Magnus term is C_M*rho*V*(omega × v), so C_M = 3k/8; Steyn's k = 0.195 corresponds to C_M ≈ 0.073, far below the default 1.0. A constant CL ≈ 0.2 (the TWU backspin finding) cannot be represented this way at all because MuJoCo's Magnus force is linear in omega.
- Therefore prefer computing aerodynamic force yourself each step (via `xfrc_applied` or a passive-force callback): F = -(0.5*rho*Cd*A)*|v_rel|*v_rel + (0.5*rho*A*CL(S, sign))*|v_rel|^2*(omega_hat × v_hat), with v_rel = v - wind, and leave MuJoCo's fluid model off. This also sidesteps having to verify how the blunt-drag coefficient maps onto CD for a sphere (not checked here).
- Ball inertia: model the ball as a thin spherical shell, I = (2/3)*m*r^2 ≈ 2.2e-5 kg·m^2 for m = 24.3 g, r = 0.037 m (set explicitly via `inertial`; a default solid sphere geom would give (2/5)*m*r^2 and get spin transfer at bounces wrong by a factor of 5/3 in the inertia term).
- Added mass is negligible: 0.5*rho*V ≈ 0.13 g, about 0.5% of ball mass (and the perforated shell makes even that uncertain).
- Tunnelling and timestep: at a ball-paddle closing speed of 25 m/s the ball moves 25 mm per 1 ms step, more than a thin paddle face's thickness and comparable to the ball radius (37 mm), so default timesteps of 2 ms can miss or badly mis-resolve the contact. To resolve a roughly 4 ms real contact with the solref constraint (time constant ≥ 2 × timestep) and several steps per contact, the timestep needs to be of order 0.1 to 0.25 ms during contacts. Options: (a) global small timestep (costly for RL); (b) sub-step only near predicted contacts; (c) thicken the paddle collision geom (box with depth behind the face) and use `margin` so contact activates early; (d) preferably, detect contact geometrically (swept sphere against paddle plane/court plane/net plane between steps) and apply the calibrated impulse map directly to ball velocity and spin, bypassing the soft-contact solver for the ball. Option (d) makes the simulator's bounce exactly equal to the fitted model.
- If MuJoCo contact is kept for the court bounce, the restitution that results from solref depends on timestep and impact speed, so calibrate by simulated drop tests (78 in drop should rebound 30 to 34 in) and re-check across the speed range; use `condim` 3 or higher with elliptic cones so the friction impulse and spin change are represented, and verify the grip/slide behaviour against the impulse model.
- Net: a thin mesh/plane with a fast ball has the same tunnelling problem; treat it as an event (ball centre crossing the net plane below net height plus radius) with a heavily damped response (low COR), and handle net-cord dribbles as a stochastic outcome unless they matter for the task. No measured net restitution was found.
- Use RK4 or an implicit integrator for flight; **[TT]** evidence shows RK4 is accurate to sub-centimetre at large steps for ball flight, while explicit Euler is not.

- MuJoCo documentation sections on multiccd, integrator details and any explicit statement on continuous collision detection were not reached (page truncated); from background knowledge MuJoCo has no general continuous collision detection for fast bodies, but this was not confirmed in this session.
- The exact relation between MuJoCo's blunt-drag coefficient and a sphere's CD was not verified.
- No published MuJoCo pickleball model or parameter set was found; no data on pickleball net interaction (restitution, friction) was found.
