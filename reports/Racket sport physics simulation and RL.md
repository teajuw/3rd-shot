# Simulate the Ball First, Bodies Later

Yes, the reconstruction can and should become a physics simulation, but in layers, and the cheapest layer is the most valuable one. A calibrated, event-based ball simulator (flight with drag and lift, friction bounces, a paddle impact map) can be fitted to the footage on the Mac with no rented compute, and it directly improves the reconstruction it is fitted to. Torque-controlled bodies that track the recovered motion are feasible but belong later: every published tracker trains on NVIDIA GPUs, video-derived motion is only about 45 to 63% trackable before clean-up, and no one has run any of it on pickleball or on SAM 3D Body output at scale. Reinforcement-learning agents that play, and that learn player style from new video, have a documented recipe in tennis (Vid2Player3D, AdaPT) costing roughly 1 to 60 GPU-hours per skill and 1.5 to 4.5 hours of motion per player for a full style model; a four-player doubles agent with free choice of who hits has not been published in any racket sport. Unity with ML-Agents and the SMPL Unity package are the wrong core: use MuJoCo on the Mac, the same model file on a rented NVIDIA GPU for training, and Unity or three.js only as a display. The shot-target predictor the reader wants does **not** need the physics simulation. It is a supervised model over the hit and bounce log, with published precedents in tennis and badminton that used roughly 2,000 to 3,500 shots per player or 34,000 to 43,000 strokes across about 30 players; what it needs is volume of reconstructed rallies, and the simulation's contribution is cleaner labels and a feasibility mask. Nothing in this report has been demonstrated on pickleball: there is no published pickleball physics simulation, RL agent, shot-forecasting model, wind-tunnel measurement, or court friction coefficient.

Throughout, "measured" or a bare citation means a figure reported by the cited source; "inference" means reasoning in the research notes with no source behind it; "judgement" marks this report's own recommendation. Several figures reached the notes through search-result summaries; those are flagged where they carry weight.

## Three simulations hide inside the question, and they cost very different amounts

The existing reconstruction already contains what the tennis literature calls the kinematic dataset: per-player body motion placed on the court, contact times, and a ball path. Vid2Player3D, the one fully documented system that goes from single-camera broadcast video to physically simulated racket-sport players, has four stages: kinematic motion from video, a physics tracker that imitates that motion in simulation to produce a corrected dataset, a motion model (a VAE) fitted to the corrected motion, and a high-level policy that steers the motion model and directly controls the wrist ([Vid2Player3D paper](https://research.nvidia.com/labs/toronto-ai/vid2player3d/data/tennis_skills_main.pdf)). The reader has stage one. The question is how many of the remaining stages are worth building, and in what order.

It helps to separate three things that "physics simulation" could mean here.

| Layer | What is simulated | What it buys | Where it runs |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Ball and contacts | Flight ODE, court bounce, net, paddle impact as discrete events | Better 3D ball fits, spin estimates, counterfactual shots, a training environment for paddle-level agents | Mac CPU |
| B. Kinematic players plus physical ball | Bodies replay or retrieve reconstructed motion; paddle and ball obey physics | A watchable, steppable twin; reach and timing questions; web delivery | Mac CPU, browser |
| C. Torque-controlled bodies | Humanoids driven by a learned controller that tracks the recovered motion and stays upright | Physically corrected motion, bodies that can respond to new balls, full RL agents | Rented NVIDIA GPU to train; Mac to replay |

The literature supports starting at A and B. Shot-level and tracking-level models with no bodies already reproduce serve speeds, rally lengths and win percentages, and answer counterfactuals: SportsNGEN treats tennis players as 2D points and still simulates whole matches ([SportsNGEN](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.12977v3)). A robot arm with eight controlled joints and 17 small skill networks reached amateur human level at table tennis ([DeepMind robot table tennis](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.03906v1)). Top systems simplify ball physics freely: Vid2Player3D precomputes ball trajectories as a lookup table, a SIGGRAPH 2024 table-tennis character omits the Magnus effect, and PingpongDuo ignores spin ([Vid2Player3D supplement](https://cs.stanford.edu/~haotianz/vid2player3d/data/tennis_skills_supp.pdf); [Wang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.16210v1); [PingpongDuo](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.20579)). Full bodies, by contrast, demanded a large extra layer in every system just to hit the ball reliably: plain PPO on a humanoid produced unnatural tennis swings in SMPLOlympics and failed to converge at all in LATENT ([SMPLOlympics](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.00187); [LATENT](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.12686)).

Layer C is still worth reaching, for a specific reason. Physics tracking is the established way to turn smoothed video kinematics into dynamically consistent motion. Vid2Player3D measured it stage by stage for one player: raw video motion had jitter of 6.08 (10^3 m/s^3) and **7.41 cm of foot sliding; after physics correction, 3.14 and 1.46 cm** ([Vid2Player3D paper](https://research.nvidia.com/labs/toronto-ai/vid2player3d/data/tennis_skills_main.pdf)). It is also the only route to bodies that react to a ball that was not in the video. But it is the expensive layer, the one with licence strings, and the one with no pickleball evidence, so it should follow the layers that pay for themselves.

The overall pipeline, then, is: calibrated camera and court; 2D ball and player tracks; 3D ball arcs with hits and bounces and SAM 3D Body motion placed on the court (all existing); then (A) identify ball, bounce and paddle parameters by matching simulated trajectories to the pixels; (B) build one MuJoCo scene with court, net, four bodies with welded paddles and the event-driven ball, first replayed kinematically; (C) convert each player's motion to a simulated humanoid, run a pretrained tracker to find which segments it can follow, then fine-tune; and finally train task policies on top. Each step consumes the previous step's output and none requires discarding earlier work.

## The sim-to-video gap closes at the ball: fit physics to pixels and treat contacts as events

**What the current ball model is missing.** The reader's drag coefficient of 0.33 matches the best available free-flight measurement for an outdoor 40-hole ball, which found a mean of 0.33 with a standard deviation of 0.083 between shots, against about 0.45 for indoor 26-hole balls ([TWU pickleball aerodynamics](https://twu.tennis-warehouse.com/learning_center/pickleball/pickleball_aerodynamics.php)). A second study fitted 0.30 ± 0.02 from six trajectories ([Steyn et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.00163v1)). The missing term is lift. The same free-flight study measured lift coefficients of about -0.24 for topspin and +0.17 to 0.2 for backspin, with the backspin value nearly independent of spin rate and a non-zero lift at zero spin; Steyn et al. instead fit lift proportional to spin, with "large scatter" and an internal inconsistency. The notes calculate that a lift coefficient of 0.2 at 10 m/s is about a fifth of the ball's weight, enough to move a one-second flight by roughly a metre against a no-spin arc (inference). The two published lift forms differ by up to a factor of about five at low spin, so lift is a parameter to fit and randomise, not a constant to look up.

**The bounce is where spin becomes visible.** The reader's restitution of 0.66 is the top of the certification window: a 78-inch drop onto granite must rebound 30 to 34 inches, a speed ratio of 0.62 to 0.66, at an impact speed near 6 m/s ([RPM ball science](https://rpmpb.com/pages/ball-science)). Table-tennis work finds restitution falls with impact speed and calls a constant value a "major heuristic" ([arXiv 2608.08157](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.08157)). No measured friction coefficient for a pickleball on an acrylic court exists in the sources found. The notes recommend the slide-or-grip impulse model used in robot table tennis and Rod Cross's tennis work, applied to a thin hollow shell: above an incidence angle of roughly 26 degrees a spinless ball grips, leaves with about three fifths of its horizontal speed, and picks up topspin (inference, from standard rigid-body mechanics with a placeholder friction of 0.5). The practical consequence is that every bounce yields two observable numbers, the vertical ratio and the horizontal speed change, and the second depends directly on incoming spin regardless of aerodynamics.

**The paddle face is not frictionless.** Certification bounds the paddle model from two sides: PBCoR at most 0.43 with a ball fired at 60 mph at a pivot-mounted paddle, and at most 2,100 rpm in a 40 mph, 30-degree oblique test ([USA Pickleball PBCoR](https://usapickleball.org/?p=62320); [Pickleball Science on the spin test](https://pickleballscience.org/analysis-of-usap-spin-rate-test/)). A test engineer quoted in the latter says that at 45 degrees and steeper all paddles give about the same spin because the ball does not slip. That means the change in ball velocity at a hit is not parallel to the face normal, which is the assumption behind setting the paddle face from the ball. The notes estimate the resulting face-angle error at up to about 16 to 27 degrees on sliding contacts (inference). For scale, the only ground-truth-validated figure for racket orientation recovered from monocular broadcast video by impact inversion is 26.4 ± 4.4 degrees in table tennis ([TT4D](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.01234)). The fix is the same impulse model in the paddle frame, with paddle velocity taken from the wrist track and outgoing spin constrained by the following flight and bounce.

**How to calibrate, given only passive footage.** Robot table tennis has a settled recipe: a small physics core whose few parameters are identified from tracked trajectories, with learned corrections added only where physics is known to be weak, and contacts handled as explicit reset maps between smooth flight phases instead of time-stepped collisions. The event-based formulation was 4.1 times faster than fixed-step simulation at similar accuracy, and RK4 kept flight error under a centimetre at large steps ([arXiv 2608.08157](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.08157)). Racket contact is improved by residual networks or Gaussian processes over the impulse parameters ([arXiv 2604.11349](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.11349)). The notes' transfer to this project (inference) has four parts. Fit globally shared parameters (a lumped drag term per ball type, a lift function, restitution as a function of normal speed, court friction, paddle restitution and friction) jointly with per-segment initial state and spin. Score the fit by **2D reprojection error in pixels**, not against the already-reconstructed 3D track, because that track assumed a physics model and would bias the fit toward it. Hold the camera fixed from court lines first, since a mis-scaled court looks exactly like a drag error. Add residual models only after checking that residuals do not depend on image position or depth, which would mean they are absorbing calibration error.

**How much gap will remain.** Two findings set expectations. Even in a calibrated robot lab with dedicated cameras, a table-tennis system with near-zero simulated error had **13.1 ± 7.3 cm landing error** on the real robot, attributed to perception and calibration, not dynamics ([arXiv 2608.08157](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.08157)). And monocular spin estimation has only been validated for sign: networks trained purely on simulated trajectories classify topspin versus backspin at 89.5 to 97.1% on broadcast table tennis, with no magnitude error reported because no ground truth exists ([Uplifting Table Tennis](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.20250v1); [Kienzle et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.19863)). The notes expect reliable spin sign on drives, serves and drops in pickleball, magnitude to within several hundred rpm on long flights at best, and nothing useful on dinks and fast volley exchanges (inference; no pickleball benchmark exists). Better ball detection near hits and bounces will likely shrink the gap more than physics terms beyond lift and a friction bounce.

**Validation.** The nearest thing to a sim-to-real loop with passive video is held-out replay: start the simulator at a real hit and compare the simulated continuation with what the camera saw. Useful metrics are landing-point error, time of flight, net clearance, and open-loop prediction of one segment from the previous one, plus distribution-level comparisons of rally length and bounce depth. The held-out rallies should be a random sample, not hand-picked ones; hand-picked hard cases are a separate test suite. For anything trained inside the simulator, randomise over what the video cannot pin down: the notes suggest drag by ±12 to 25%, lift across the range between the two published forms, restitution 0.60 to 0.70 with a speed slope, friction ranges, and wind outdoors (inference).

**In MuJoCo specifically.** MuJoCo's built-in fluid model has a Magnus term linear in spin and cannot express a lift coefficient with an intercept or a topspin/backspin asymmetry, so aerodynamic forces should be applied as custom forces with the fluid model off ([MuJoCo fluid forces](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/stable/computation/fluid.html)). Soft contacts cannot resolve an impact of a few milliseconds without sub-millisecond timesteps, so restitution and spin transfer are better imposed by detecting the contact geometrically and applying the calibrated impulse map directly; that makes the simulator's bounce exactly equal to the fitted model (inference). MuJoCo 4.0.0, released 6 November 2026, adds speculative contacts that stop fast convex bodies tunnelling through thin obstacles, which lowers the risk if engine contact is used for the paddle ([MuJoCo changelog](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/changelog.html)). Model the ball as a thin shell, not the default solid sphere, or spin transfer at bounces is wrong by the ratio of the two inertias (inference).

**The body-side gap.** For the players, the gap is between what the video shows and what a balanced simulated body can do. Universal trackers reach 98.9 to 100% success on cleaned motion capture ([PHC](https://github.com/ZhengyiLuo/PHC)), but video-derived motion is much harder: only 62.82% of GVHMR outputs on about 4,000 internet videos could be tracked by a physics controller ([FlowHMR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03691)), and tracking failure on two scene-interaction benchmarks was 55.2% until scene geometry was cleaned ([CRISP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.14696)). Using a tracker as a plain post-filter also has a cost: passing GVHMR output through PHC+ cut floating artefacts but **more than doubled foot sliding (5.65 to 12.71 mm on EMDB2)** and worsened joint error ([PhysHMR](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.02566v1)). A flat court removes the scene-geometry problem, so pickleball should sit at the easier end; fast lunges, split steps and occlusion glitches are the likely failures (inference). Three practices carry over from the tennis work: pretrain or reuse a tracker trained on motion capture and fine-tune on the sport's clips; allow residual root forces at first (Vid2Player3D found removing them cut foot sliding from 1.46 to 0.87 cm but dropped hit rate from 0.89 to 0.78); and do not trust the wrist. Vid2Player3D, LATENT and AdaPT each hand wrist angles near contact to the task policy, and without that correction Vid2Player3D's bounce-in rate fell from 0.85 to 0.46 and LATENT's forehand success from 96.52% to 82.36% ([Vid2Player3D paper](https://research.nvidia.com/labs/toronto-ai/vid2player3d/data/tennis_skills_main.pdf); [LATENT](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.12686); [AdaPT](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.20087)). The reader's approximate paddle is therefore an adequate start, and the paddle face at contact should eventually be solved by a controller constrained by the observed ball outcome, not read from pose.

## RL agents can play and can learn from new video, with a known recipe and a doubles-shaped hole

**The architecture is settled.** Every published sports agent that plays whole points uses a low-level controller learned by imitating human motion and a high-level policy that sees the ball and the opponent and issues a compact command, often only once per incoming ball. The command is either a continuous latent of a motion prior (Vid2Player3D, DeepMind football, LATENT) or a discrete skill plus a landing target (DeepMind table tennis, Wang et al., PingpongDuo) ([DeepMind football](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2105.12196); [DeepMind robot table tennis](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.03906v1); [Wang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.16210v1)). The second family lines up with the reader's data: a log of hits, hitter positions and bounce locations is a dataset of high-level actions (inference). A shot-level model trained on that log can later serve as the high-level policy of a physical agent.

**Three recipes, in increasing cost.** The cheapest uses one or two reference swings per stroke and a goal-conditioned tracker with a model-based ball predictor: TaskNPoint generalised a single video demonstration per skill to new targets in under one GPU-hour on an RTX 4090, with 91% success over 34 motions in simulation, though without simulated object contact in training; HITTER used two video-derived swings ([TaskNPoint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.26215); [HITTER](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.21043)). The middle recipe uses hours of motion fragments, a latent skill space and a task policy: LATENT used five hours of amateur motion capture and eight GPUs ([LATENT](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.12686)). The full recipe learns player-specific style from broadcast video: Vid2Player3D used 80 to 103 minutes of motion per player from 13 US Open broadcasts and trained in about 12 hours for the tracker, four for the motion model and two days for the high-level policy on one data-centre GPU; AdaPT used 179 to 267 minutes per player on four RTX 4090s ([Vid2Player3D paper](https://research.nvidia.com/labs/toronto-ai/vid2player3d/data/tennis_skills_main.pdf); [AdaPT](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.20087)). Vid2Player3D's controllers reached a median hit rate of 0.92 and bounce-in rate of 0.85, with bounce position error of 1.5 to 1.9 m on a tennis court, and two independently trained controllers sustained a 38-shot rally.

**Learning from new videos.** The proven pattern is a generic model plus a per-player fine-tune or identity embedding: Vid2Player3D fine-tunes its tracker per player with about a quarter of the pretraining samples, SportsNGEN adds a learned identity vector with unseen players falling back to a generic one, and the badminton models use player embeddings ([Vid2Player3D supplement](https://cs.stanford.edu/~haotianz/vid2player3d/data/tennis_skills_supp.pdf); [SportsNGEN](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.12977v3)). New matches therefore add value incrementally. The data requirement is the catch. With about ten minutes of one player's motion, Vid2Player3D's hit and bounce-in rates fell significantly "because the learned motion embedding is not dense enough". One pickleball match yields far less motion per player than that, so a per-player style model needs many matches of the same player, or pooling across players on top of a general motion prior (inference). At the tactical level the requirements are lighter: a heuristic table-tennis opponent was built from 20 minutes of broadcast video, and offline imitation of badminton players (RallyNet) used 43,191 strokes from 31 players, beating plain behaviour cloning on every rollout metric ([Wang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.16210v1); [RallyNet](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.12406v2)).

**Self-play is optional and expensive.** Population-based self-play with simulated bodies has been done at scale once, for 2v2 football: 8×10^10 environment steps over about six weeks on 4,096 CPU actors, with performance still improving ([DeepMind football](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2105.12196)). Racket-sport work has avoided it. Agents train against ball launchers, fixed heuristic opponents or a frozen copy; LATENT's "self-play" is two copies of one return policy; DeepMind's table-tennis robot reached amateur level through seven train-deploy-collect cycles with human opponents and no self-play. The recurring failures are cold start (neither side can return the ball, so there is no reward), collapse onto one stroke or court region, overfitting to the current opponent, and an imitation reward that overpowers the task: SMPLOlympics' adversarial-prior tennis agent got the most hits but a 5.30 m landing error ([SMPLOlympics](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.00187)). For a twin whose goal is fidelity to real players, tactics should come from data. Self-play only matters if the goal becomes finding strategies no one plays.

**Doubles is unpublished territory.** No paper found has a four-body, physically simulated racket game with two players per side and free choice of who hits. PingpongDuo, the first humanoid table-tennis doubles system, has two robots alternating strikes by rule, no learned opponent, and needed a joint positioning planner with a learned collision-risk filter to stop teammates colliding; its learned planner raised mean consecutive returns from 3.79 to 4.87 ([PingpongDuo](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.20579)). SMPLOlympics has no doubles racket environment and says its 2v2 team baselines fall short. For replay, doubles adds little: each player tracks their own clip and one player touches the ball per shot. It becomes research when agents must decide who takes a middle ball and where to stand. Pickleball's own rules (two-bounce rule, non-volley zone, side-out scoring, underhand serve) appear in no learning paper found.

## MuJoCo on the Mac and a rented NVIDIA GPU beat Unity for everything except display

**Unity with ML-Agents is the wrong training core.** ML-Agents is alive but in low-intensity maintenance: the last tagged GitHub release is Release 23 (package 4.0.0, August 2025), and later point releases are dependency updates ([ML-Agents releases](https://github.com/Unity-Technologies/ml-agents/releases)). It has no motion-imitation tooling, no SMPL-shaped physics humanoid and no motion-capture pipeline, and the modern humanoid literature (PHC, PULSE, MaskedMimic, SMPLOlympics, ProtoMotions, mjlab) runs entirely on Isaac or MuJoCo-family simulators. The scale difference is stark: a 2019 Unity humanoid backflip took about 25 hours with 64 parallel agents, while an Isaac Gym humanoid policy trained in about six minutes with 4,096 environments on one GPU ([Marathon Environments](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.09097); [Isaac Gym paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.10470)). Choosing Unity means rebuilding that stack alone (inference). Unity does fit as a presentation layer or a VR front end, which is how the SIGGRAPH 2024 table-tennis work used it, and MuJoCo ships an official Unity plug-in as a bridge.

**The SMPL Unity package is a skin, not a body.** The Max Planck SMPL and SMPL-X Unity packages are rigged meshes with blend shapes and animation set-up scripts under non-commercial research licences; nothing in their descriptions provides colliders, masses, joint limits or actuators ([SMPL-X for Blender and Unity](https://ps.is.mpg.de/code/smpl-x-for-blender-and-unity); [SMPL-X licence](https://smpl-x.is.tue.mpg.de/modellicense.html)). A physics humanoid needs exactly those, and the tool that derives them from SMPL shape is SMPLSim, which writes MuJoCo model files and is BSD-3 licensed ([SMPLSim](https://github.com/ZhengyiLuo/SMPLSim)). No generator that builds a physics humanoid directly from MHR parameters was found. The two routes from SAM 3D Body output are Meta's official MHR-to-SMPL converter followed by SMPLSim, or skipping SMPL and fitting MHR keypoints onto an existing MuJoCo humanoid by inverse kinematics. A personal project called Holo does the second end to end (video, SAM 3D Body, MuJoCo retarget at about 2 cm per joint, Meta Motivo tracking) and self-reports 5.3 to 9.1 cm posture error without falls on two clips ([MHR conversion tool](https://github.com/facebookresearch/MHR/blob/main/tools/mhr_smpl_conversion/README.md); [Holo](https://github.com/Harp404/Holo)). For the paddle, copy the universal practice: weld it to the wrist with a fixed per-player grip offset, as SMPLOlympics and Vid2Player3D do; articulated fingers remain unfinished in mainstream trackers.

The strongest argument for MuJoCo is continuity: one model file serves Mac development, GPU training through MuJoCo Warp, mjlab, Newton or ProtoMotions, and the browser, where Google DeepMind maintains WebAssembly bindings that can replay or step the scene live ([MuJoCo wasm README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google-deepmind/mujoco/main/wasm/README.md)). The research trend points the same way: racket-sport humanoid work moved from Isaac Gym (2023 to 2024) to Isaac Lab (2025) to MuJoCo-based mjlab and MuJoCo Playground (2026).

**Compute.** The MJX documentation lists a native-MuJoCo humanoid figure for an Apple M3 Max that is probably about 650,000 steps per second, though the notes could not map the table's numbers to devices with certainty, against roughly 3 million for MuJoCo Warp on an NVIDIA GPU ([MJX docs](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/stable/mjx.html)). The notes infer the Mac is 5 to 20 times slower before accounting for a four-humanoid scene; a local benchmark is trivial and should replace that estimate. Published training budgets span four orders of magnitude: a single skill or clip in 15 to 60 minutes on one RTX 4090; a general tracker over all of AMASS in about 12 hours on four A100s; frontier trackers at 9,000 to 32,000 GPU-hours ([ProtoMotions](https://github.com/NVlabs/ProtoMotions); [SONIC](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.07820v1)). Aggregator sites put 2026 rental prices at roughly $0.34 to $0.74 per hour for an RTX 4090 and $1.19 to $1.79 for an A100 80 GB, with disagreement between sites ([RunPod pricing](https://www.runpod.io/gpu-cloud/pricing)). On that arithmetic a single-clip policy costs under a dollar, a full-AMASS tracker roughly $60 to $90, and a Vid2Player3D-scale player model on the order of $100 (the notes' arithmetic). The cheapest path is not to train a general tracker at all, but to reuse a released one and fine-tune.

**Licences.** These decide what can be shared. SMPL and SMPL-X are non-commercial and prohibit training models for commercial purposes; every checkpoint trained on AMASS with the SMPL body (PHC, PULSE, MaskedMimic's SMPL models, Meta Motivo) inherits that, and Motivo and HumEnv are CC BY-NC 4.0 directly ([SMPL-X licence](https://smpl-x.is.tue.mpg.de/modellicense.html); [HumEnv](https://github.com/facebookresearch/humenv)). Vid2Player3D's code is non-commercial under an NVIDIA licence, and its trained models were withheld "due to license issues of the video footage", a caution for anything trained on broadcast footage of named professionals ([Vid2Player3D licence](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/nv-tlabs/vid2player3d/main/LICENSE.txt); [repo](https://github.com/nv-tlabs/vid2player3d)). The clean route is MHR (Apache-2.0) to a hand-scripted MuJoCo body, trained with ProtoMotions' Apache-2.0 code on own or permissively licensed motion; it forgoes every SMPL-body pretrained checkpoint ([MHR](https://github.com/facebookresearch/MHR)). For a personal research twin the non-commercial tools are fine. This is a reading of licence texts, not legal advice.

## Predicting shot targets as a distribution is a data problem, and the simulator is a helper

**What the literature has done.** Placement predictors exist for tennis and badminton, and they consume what the reconstruction already produces: incoming ball, both players' positions, hitter identity and rally history. In tennis, a memory-augmented GAN trained on 8,780 Hawk-Eye shots from three players reached a mean landing error of 0.93 m, against 1.09 m without per-player adaptation and 1.89 m for an earlier dynamic Bayesian network ([Fernando et al.](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1901.05123)). In badminton, ShuttleNet outputs a single bivariate Gaussian for the landing point from 43,191 strokes by 31 players; RallyNet outputs a mixture of bivariate Gaussians for landing and post-shot movement; SportsNGEN predicts a categorical distribution over a discretised grid and obtains placement distributions by rolling the model forward many times ([ShuttleNet](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2112.01044); [RallyNet](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.12406v2); [SportsNGEN](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.12977v3)). For "several targets are plausible", the two representations with racket-sport precedent are a mixture density head and a court-grid heatmap; a single Gaussian cannot express "cross-court or down the line", and GAN samples give no likelihood. No diffusion-based landing predictor was found.

**What the inputs are worth.** The ablations point one way. Rally history and both players' positions carry most of the signal. Hitter identity gives a moderate gain for placement and a large gain for shot type: removing player style from ShuttleNet worsened shot-type cross-entropy by about 22% but landing error only slightly. Body pose has thin evidence. The one clean ablation, RallyTemPose on badminton with 2D skeletons and shot type as the target, found that "the most critical factor is the inclusion of the player ground position" and that pose added a few accuracy points ([RallyTemPose](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024W/CVsports/papers/Ibh_A_Stroke_of_Genius_Predicting_the_Next_Move_in_Badminton_CVPRW_2024_paper.pdf)). A 2026 preprint found 3D pose coordinates far better than image landmarks for tennis shot direction across players, but compared pose representations, not pose against no pose ([arXiv 2606.15992](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.15992), via search summary). **No source measures whether pre-contact 3D pose or paddle orientation improves landing-location prediction**, which is exactly the signal the reader has and others lacked. The notes distinguish two problems (inference): a tactical forecast made as the incoming ball arrives, from positions, history and identity; and anticipation in the last few hundred milliseconds before contact, where the hitter's body and paddle should matter. One model conditioned on time-to-contact can cover both, and measuring how the distribution sharpens as contact approaches would be a new result.

**Does it need the physics simulation?** No. Every published predictor is a supervised model on tracked or charted data. The simulation helps in three indirect ways (judgement). The calibrated ball model produces cleaner landing points and outgoing velocities, which are the labels. It supplies a feasibility mask: targets that cannot clear the net or stay in court from the contact point at plausible speeds can be given zero probability, and physics constrains what is reachable outside the data distribution, which SportsNGEN lists as its weakness. And it lets a predicted target be turned into a simulated shot for counterfactual replay. None of these is required for a first model.

**What data it needs.** The precedents suggest a ladder: about 2,000 to 3,500 shots per player supported a player-specific tennis model, and 34,000 to 43,000 strokes across 26 to 31 players supported the badminton models. A pickleball court is far smaller than a tennis court, so metre-scale error would be much less useful; accuracy by zone relative to court size is the fairer yardstick (inference). No published shot-by-shot forecasting model, and no confirmed public tracked dataset, exists for pickleball. The public source closest to usable is pklmart, a manually charted professional dataset with rally and shot tables including shot type and location fields, available through the `pklshop` package; its full column dictionary and licence were not verified ([pklshop docs](https://nolansmyth.github.io/pklshop/index.html)). The sensible path is a generic model pooled across all players with a player embedding, pretrained on charted data if its location fields prove usable, then per-player adaptation as reconstructed matches accumulate. Doubles adds a target the literature does not model: which opponent is attacked, and middle versus line.

**How to evaluate it.** Published practice is weak for distributions. ShuttleNet scores landing error on the best of ten samples, which uses the ground truth to choose and rewards sharp guesses; most others report mean Euclidean error. No paper found reports log-likelihood or calibration for per-shot landing distributions; SportsNGEN's win-probability calibration plot is the only calibration evidence. Held-out negative log-likelihood, reliability curves and top-k zone accuracy would improve on the field, not replicate it. Hold out whole matches chosen at random; the badminton papers instead split chronologically within each match, which puts the same match on both sides.

## What a working twin makes possible, and which research to copy

Research systems have already demonstrated each application on other sports. Counterfactual replay and shot recommendation: SportsNGEN branches a real rally at a shot and estimates win probability for each alternative, finding about 58% for cross-court corner shots against under 50% for the middle shot actually played. Tactic search: Match Point AI runs tree search over shot directions against data-derived opponents ([Match Point AI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.05960)). Opponent scouting and style comparison from player embeddings. A sparring partner: a human played the simulated table-tennis agent in VR, with the skill controller fine-tuned on that user's play ([Wang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.16210v1)). Synthetic training data for perception: table-tennis uplifting networks were trained on 140,000 simulated trajectories, which becomes available for pickleball once the simulator is calibrated ([Uplifting Table Tennis](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.20250v1)). Physically corrected motion with less foot sliding. And, further out, humanoid robots: the same pipeline shape now drives real tennis, table tennis and badminton robots.

Commercially, pickleball video analytics exists (PB Vision, integrated with DUPR ratings in 2026, per search summaries), but no product found sells a simulated, playable or counterfactual twin of a match ([DUPR, January 2026](https://www.dupr.com/post/dupr-partners-with-pb-vision-to-enable-ai-powered-ratings)). A doubles dataset with 3D bodies, ball, hits and bounces for four players appears richer than any public doubles racket-sport dataset found, so even descriptive models of who takes middle balls or partner spacing would be new (inference).

## A staged plan, cheapest and most valuable first

The ordering is this report's judgement. Stages 1 and 2 are independent and can run in parallel; each later stage has a gate that decides whether to continue.

Stages 1 to 4 need no rented compute and no restrictively licensed component, and they deliver the calibrated simulator, the probabilistic shot predictor, a steppable twin and counterfactual replay. Stages 5 to 7 are where rented GPUs, non-commercial licences and unproven territory begin. Stopping after Stage 4 still yields a product no one has published for pickleball.

## What is unknown or unproven for pickleball

No peer-reviewed wind-tunnel measurement of a pickleball exists; all aerodynamic values are free-flight fits at 5 to 16 m/s, and nothing is measured at drive and serve speeds of 20 to 30 m/s. The two lift measurements disagree several-fold. There is no measured spin decay in flight, no court friction coefficient, no oblique-bounce study, no restitution against impact speed or temperature, no paddle friction or dwell-time measurement by high-speed video (the roughly 4 ms figure is a blog estimate), and no data on net interaction. No method or benchmark exists for spin magnitude from monocular video in any sport, and no pipeline has been published that fits simulator parameters to monocular broadcast video with reported parameter accuracy. The slide-or-grip arithmetic and randomisation ranges in this report are the notes' reasoning from tennis and table tennis.

On bodies: no source evaluates physics tracking on SAM 3D Body or MHR input beyond one personal project's two clips, none reports tracker success on racket-sport motion, none confirms any released tracker running on Apple Silicon, and the MHR-to-SMPL conversion error is undocumented. Far-side players were skipped entirely by Vid2Player3D; whether pickleball's shorter court makes them usable is untested. Per-player body shapes may lower zero-shot tracking success, since released trackers were mostly evaluated on a mean-shape body. How much smoothing before tracking helps or removes real paddle-head speed is unstudied.

On agents: no pickleball RL, self-play or imitation paper was found; no league or self-play result exists for any simulated full-body racket sport; no doubles agent with free choice of hitter exists; no paper compares paddle-only with full-body agents for tactical fidelity; no blinded "which is the real player" test has been reported. On prediction: no pickleball shot-forecasting model, no doubles placement model, no evidence either way that 3D pose or paddle orientation improves landing prediction, and no confirmed public tracked pickleball dataset. Mac throughput for a four-humanoid scene, WebAssembly performance, cloud prices and several repository licences (PHC's own terms, AMASS, the LATENT and AdaPT code status) were not verified and should be checked at the point of use.

## Conclusion

The research inverts the order the question implies. The natural reading is reconstruction, then simulation, then agents, then prediction as the final payoff. The evidence says the prediction model and the ball simulator are both first-stage work that need neither bodies nor GPUs, and that the body simulation is the optional, expensive branch whose main near-term product is cleaner motion. That reordering matters because the binding constraint for the reader's stated goal is not physics or compute. It is the number of reconstructed shots, and every hour spent making the reconstruction pipeline run unattended over more matches buys more predictive power than any simulator feature.

Two things are new enough to be worth doing carefully. The first is spin: pickleball bounces expose spin through the horizontal speed change, the reconstruction already has bounces, and nobody has published a monocular spin estimate for the sport, so a jointly fitted flight-and-bounce model would produce data that does not exist elsewhere. The second is the pose ablation for shot targets. The field has speculated for years that 3D body and racket state should sharpen placement prediction near contact and has never measured it, mostly because nobody had 3D bodies, a paddle and a ball in the same dataset. This project does, and a calibrated answer to "how much does the distribution narrow in the last 300 milliseconds, and for which players" is both the feature the reader wants and a result the literature lacks.
