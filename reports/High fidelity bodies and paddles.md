# Anchor SAM 3D Body, Infer the Paddle

SAM 3D Body is a real option and the best starting point for higher-fidelity bodies, but only as a per-frame pose source wrapped in your own temporal and court-grounding layer; SAM 3D Objects is not a workable way to track the paddle. No alternative is clearly better: SAM 3D Body leads every SMPL-family model on per-frame accuracy, it has two measured Apple Silicon paths (roughly 0.2 to 0.5 s per person per frame), and it outputs Meta's MHR body, which is the only leading option whose mesh can be embedded in a web page without breaching a body-model licence. Its weaknesses (no temporal model, noisy depth) are exactly what the existing calibration and court-plane foot tracks already solve. The paddle has no off-the-shelf monocular 6-DoF estimator in any racket sport, so it should be a known rigid model attached to the wrist, with face angle set by ball-impact physics at each hit and refined from paddle keypoints or masks on sharp near-side frames. Expect near players to look recognisably right in body pose with paddle face angles good to roughly 15 to 30 degrees at hits, and far players (80 to 150 px) to show plausible but visibly coarser poses with prior-driven wrists and paddles. Full-rate processing costs about 45 to 100 minutes of compute per minute of rally video on measured Apple Silicon numbers, which drops to roughly 15 to 40 minutes with reduced frame rates. No published source measures any of these models on people this small or on pickleball, so a 30-second benchmark on your own footage is the first step and the decision gate.

In this report, "measured" means a number reported by a named source, "inference" means reasoning in the research notes without a source behind it, and "judgement" marks this report's own recommendation.

## SAM 3D Body leads per frame, and its gaps are the ones the court already fills

**What it is.** SAM 3D Body (3DB) is Meta's single-image, promptable full-body mesh recovery model, released 19 November 2025 with two gated checkpoints (DINOv3-H+ at 840M parameters, ViT-H at 631M). It takes a person crop resized to 512 px, accepts optional 2D keypoint and mask prompts, and outputs Momentum Human Rig (MHR) parameters for body, feet and hands ([sam-3d-body README](https://github.com/facebookresearch/sam-3d-body); [arXiv 2602.15989](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.15989)). MHR separates skeleton from surface shape, has 45 identity and 204 pose parameters, and at the level of detail 3DB uses has **18,439 vertices against SMPL's 6,890** ([MHR README](https://github.com/facebookresearch/MHR); [Fast SAM 3D Body](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15603)). No video version or successor had been released as of November 2026.

**Why it is the right backbone.** On EMDB, 3DB reports 38.2 to 38.5 mm PA-MPJPE and 61.7 to 62.9 mm MPJPE, ahead of the image models NLF, PromptHMR and CameraHMR and of the video models GENMO (42.5 / 73.0), TRAM and WHAM, even though it sees one frame at a time. Its margin is widest where racket sport lives: on the paper's "sports or athletic pose" category it scores 85.1 body and 82.8 feet aPCK against 78.9 / 69.3 for CameraHMR, and on the extreme-pose LSPET set 68.9 PCK against 55.6 for the next best ([arXiv 2602.15989](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.15989)). These are Meta's own tables; no independent head-to-head with temporal metrics exists.

**What it lacks, and why that matters less here.** 3DB processes each person in each frame independently. Third-party wrappers all report the same symptoms on video (jitter, fluctuating shape and bone scale, noisy root depth, missed detections of small or blurred people) and all add the same fixes: lock shape per track, smooth in MHR parameter space, and solve the root trajectory with a foot-contact model ([SAM-Body4D](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08406v1); [world-coordinate retargeting](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.21573v1)). None publishes quantitative temporal results, and the retargeting paper has no code. The independent CalTennis benchmark shows that purpose-built video models do not escape the hard part either: running without a known camera, PromptHMR, GVHMR, GENMO, WHAM and TRAM had **translation errors of 0.9 to 3.6 m** on tennis, and "absolute distance and depth, ground-contact detection, and body shape" stayed unreliable for every model ([CalTennis](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.20542)). Your pipeline already knows the camera and where each player's feet are on the court, so the model is only asked for root-relative pose, where 3DB is strongest (inference in the notes, consistent across all four).

**The alternatives, and why none displaces it.** The table compares the candidates on the criteria that decide this project.

| Option | Strength | Blocking weakness here | Mac evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| SAM 3D Body + own temporal layer | Best per-frame accuracy; keypoint and mask prompts; accepts known intrinsics; MHR output | Temporal layer is your code; untested on 80-150 px people | Measured: MLX port and MPS fork |
| GVHMR (static-camera mode) | Smooth by design; lowest foot sliding among feed-forward video models (3.0 mm on static-camera RICH, 126.3 mm world MPJPE) | SMPL/SMPL-X output cannot be embedded in a page; weights described as non-commercial; accuracy falls as people get smaller | Measured: about 3.9 frames/s on an M1 Max |
| GENMO / GEM-SMPL | Lowest jitter (8.6 on RICH); generative prior helps occlusion | NVIDIA non-commercial licence; CUDA-only install documented | None |
| PromptHMR-Vid | Best local pose among released video models; full-image processing claims very small boxes | Worst on acceleration metrics and slowest in CalTennis; heavy CUDA dependency chain | None |
| InstantHMR | Distilled from 3DB, MHR output, 20-32M parameters, Core ML option | About 10 mm worse PA-MPJPE on EMDB (48.8 vs 38.2-38.5); most variants non-commercial | Advertised, unmeasured |
| DanceHMR, FactorizedHMR, HTD-Refine | Better published numbers, including hands | No confirmed code release | None |

Sources: [FactorizedHMR tables](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.14854), [CalTennis](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.20542), [atelier PR #199](https://github.com/fontanierh/atelier/pull/199), [NVlabs/GENMO](https://github.com/NVlabs/GENMO), [InstantHMR](https://github.com/Naturalpad-studio/InstantHMR), [DanceHMR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.18102), [HTD-Refine](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.26879v1).

GVHMR is the serious runner-up and the right control for a bake-off, because its temporal head gives smoothness for free. Two findings count against making it the primary. CalTennis found its error strongly tied to image scale (rank correlation -0.44, smaller people worse), and OnlineHMR's frequency analysis found it reproduces motion patterns with reduced amplitude, a warning for 50 fps swings when the model's motion prior was learned mostly from slower capture ([CalTennis](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.20542); [OnlineHMR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.17355)). Simulation-based clean-up (PHC-class trackers) is not an option on a Mac: it requires Isaac Gym and CUDA, and optimisation-based contact refinement is the feasible route ([PHC](https://github.com/ZhengyiLuo/PHC)).

**How to run 3DB in this pipeline.** Feed the YOLO11 boxes directly and the calibrated intrinsics in place of the default MoGe-2 field-of-view estimator, which removes the two slowest auxiliary stages (the notes' inference from the README and the Fast SAM 3D Body design). Use prompts selectively. The paper's ablation shows a keypoint prompt helps when its noise is under about 3% of the box, is neutral at 5% and **hurts at 10%** (EMDB MPJPE 60.1 with a clean prompt, 63.3 with none, 67.8 at 10% noise), so prompt near players with high-confidence keypoints only and leave far players unprompted or mask-prompted. Mask prompts gave the larger gains when people overlap (Hi4D MPJPE 76.4 to 47.0 mm), which is the doubles-partners-crossing case ([arXiv 2602.15989](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.15989)).

## The paddle must be inferred: wrist, impact physics, then pixels

**SAM 3D Objects is the wrong tool.** It reconstructs a masked object from one image and exports shape, texture and pose, with no video mode. Meta states it predicts objects one at a time and "isn't trained to reason about physical interactions, such as contact", takes "a few seconds" per object, and officially needs Linux and an NVIDIA GPU with at least 32 GB of VRAM; an unofficial MLX port exists, whose model card lists thin structures and soft mask edges as quality risks ([Meta AI blog](https://ai.meta.com/blog/sam-3d/); [setup.md](https://github.com/facebookresearch/sam-3d-objects/blob/main/doc/setup.md); [MLX model card](https://huggingface.co/appautomaton/sam-3d-objects-mlx-8bit)). A paddle is a thin, blurred, hand-occluded plate tens of pixels long, and a generative model would produce a slightly different shape every frame. Since paddle dimensions are known, a hand-built rigid model is simpler and more accurate (inference; no source reports SAM 3D Objects on any racket or paddle).

**Nothing off the shelf does monocular paddle pose.** The research found no published method that recovers 6-DoF racket pose from a single broadcast camera by looking at the racket, and nothing at all for pickleball. Every broadcast reconstruction system reviewed (Vid2Player3D, LATTE-MV, TT3D, TT4D) infers or synthesises the racket instead of measuring it; Vid2Player3D says plainly that wrist and racket motion are hard to estimate "due to occlusion and motion blur" ([Vid2Player3D](https://research.nvidia.com/labs/toronto-ai/vid2player3d)). The only commercial racket tracking with published detail is Hawk-Eye's multi-camera SkeleTRACK (seven racket points), and PB Vision lists paddle angle only as a long-term idea ([Hawk-Eye](https://www.hawkeyeinnovations.com/news/4243365/skeletrack-a-new-era-of-data-in-tennis); [PB Vision board](https://pbvision.featurebase.app/p/spin-rate-paddle-angle)). General 6-DoF pose stacks do not transfer: FoundationPose is built around RGB-D and CUDA, and its depth-free derivative RGBTrack loses up to half its recall on large, sharp tabletop objects ([FoundationPose](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.08344v2); [RGBTrack](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.17119v1)). Hand-object trackers fare no better: the best 2026 RGB-only one reports 19.5 to 49.2 degrees absolute rotation error on close-range benchmarks ([ComPose](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.23523)).

**The three sources of evidence that do exist.** The first is the wrist. A paddle spanning 40 to 80 px on the near side implies a hand of about 9 to 18 px, and 3 to 7 px on the far side, so finger pose is unreadable for everyone, but the wrist position from the body pipeline fixes paddle translation to within a grip offset (the notes' geometry). The second is the ball. TT4D inverts ball impact to obtain racket orientation and velocity and validated it against motion capture on 92 strokes at **26.4 ± 4.4 degrees** orientation error, the only ground-truth-validated number for racket orientation from monocular broadcast video ([TT4D](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.01234)). For pickleball the notes propose a simpler frictionless version: the face normal at contact is parallel to the ball's velocity change, and the direction from wrist to contact point fixes the handle, giving a full pose at each hit from quantities the pipeline already has. That is reasoning, not a pickleball result, and textured paddles that impart spin will bias it. The third is the image. RacketVision's five-keypoint racket detector reaches about 5 to 10 px mean error at 1080p, with the long-axis points at 93 to 99% PCK and the width points at only 65 to 80% ([RacketVision](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.17045v3)). Width is what encodes face tilt, and planar pose from points always carries a two-fold flip ambiguity that no solver can resolve ([IPPE](https://www.github.com/tobycollins/IPPE)). A 5 to 10 px error is 6 to 25% of a near paddle and 17 to 67% of a far one, so image evidence is a near-side, sharp-frame refinement and not a foundation.

**The recommended formulation (judgement, following the notes' synthesis).** Represent paddle pose as the wrist frame composed with a slowly varying per-player grip transform. Solve the grip and a smooth roll trajectory so the face meets the physics-derived normal at each hit, relaxes to a ready-position prior between shots, and, for near players, agrees with paddle masks or keypoints on frames where the paddle is sharp. Fit the known model by silhouette, anchored at the wrist, and let temporal continuity and the contact constraints choose between the flip solutions. No published system fuses these, so there is no reference accuracy for the combination. A useful side effect: the fitted paddle face gives the ball-arc fit a better contact anchor than the bare wrist.

## What near and far players will look like

No source reports mesh-recovery accuracy by person pixel height, and none evaluates on racket-sport broadcast footage, so the expectations below are bounded inference to be replaced by your own measurement.

| Aspect | Near players (roughly 170-350 px, derived from the 40-80 px paddle scale) | Far players (80-150 px) |
|---|---|---|
| Body pose | Benchmark regime; trunk, legs and arm positions recognisably right | Usable but degraded; the nearest proxy is a 3DB input-resolution ablation where 128 px input raised MPJPE from 59.0 to 86.8 mm |
| Position and footwork | Set by court-plane foot tracks; foot sliding controlled by your own stance pinning | Same mechanism, but shins can be hidden behind the net band |
| Wrists and hands | Wrist position good; forearm roll weak; hand decoder marginal at 9-18 px, use a fixed grip pose | Prior-driven; hand decoder has no signal |
| Paddle position | Within a few centimetres of the true grip, inheriting wrist error | Same, with larger wrist error |
| Paddle face at hits | Roughly 15-30 degrees from physics, tightened by image evidence on sharp frames | Roughly 15-30 degrees from physics alone; worst along the camera axis |
| Paddle between hits | Plausible, partly measured on slow frames (ready position, dinks) | Plausible only; essentially prior-driven |

Sources for the bounds: [Fast SAM 3D Body ablation](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15603), [TT4D](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.01234), [RacketVision](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.17045v3).

Three failure modes deserve advance attention. Missed detections of small or blurred people are documented for the stock 3DB pipeline, which your existing tracks avoid by supplying boxes ([SAM-Body4D](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08406v1)). Smoothing destroys real signal in fast swings, so filter strength should vary by joint and speed. And airborne phases break the flat-court assumption: Volley3D needed a takeoff and landing detector with a ballistic arc between them, which cut depth-axis error over 50 jumps from 1.05 m to 0.23 m ([Volley3D](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.38358)). Split steps and lunges will need the same treatment on a smaller scale.

## A staged plan, with compute and licence limits

**Compute on the Mac.** Two Apple Silicon paths for 3DB are measured. An MLX port merged into mlx-vlm in May 2026 runs at a median **488 ms per inference on an M3 Max**, against 667 ms for PyTorch on MPS, but supports the body only, with no hand decoder ([mlx-vlm PR #922](https://github.com/Blaizzy/mlx-vlm/pull/922)). An MPS-optimised PyTorch fork merged in October 2026 reports 349 ms per frame at batch 1 and **about 220 ms at batch 8 to 16** on an unnamed Mac, at a cost of about 2.3 mm mean joint difference; its headline "36 to 39 frames/s" is keyframed pipeline throughput, not model speed ([PoisedLabs PR #3](https://github.com/PoisedLabs/sam-3d-body/pull/3)). Neither was measured on a Mac mini. Four players at 50 fps is 12,000 person-frames per minute of video, so full-rate processing costs about 44 to 98 minutes per minute of rally. Running near players at 25 fps and far players at 12.5 fps and interpolating in joint-rotation space brings that to roughly 16 to 37 minutes per minute (this report's arithmetic). GVHMR's port measured about 3.9 frames/s per track on an M1 Max with 8.4 GB peak memory, which is about 51 minutes per minute for four tracks at full rate ([atelier PR #199](https://github.com/fontanierh/atelier/pull/199)). Memory is not a constraint at 64 GB; throughput is. Float16 on MPS is inconsistent (1.2 to 1.3x for 3DB, slower for ViTPose in the GVHMR port), and batches beyond 8 to 16 add little. Paddle physics, smoothing and root solving are CPU work that is negligible beside mesh recovery.

**Licensing.** 3DB code and checkpoints are under Meta's SAM License: royalty-free, redistribution and modification allowed, military and trade-control restrictions, an indemnity, and a clause letting Meta change terms; checkpoints are gated on Hugging Face ([LICENSE](https://github.com/facebookresearch/sam-3d-body/blob/main/LICENSE)). MHR's repository is Apache-2.0, though some mirrors label the MHR file bundled with 3DB as SAM License, so take the rig from the MHR release itself ([MHR README](https://github.com/facebookresearch/MHR); [InstantHMR](https://github.com/Naturalpad-studio/InstantHMR)). SMPL and SMPL-X are non-commercial and state the model "shall not be copied, shared, distributed", which rules out embedding their mesh and skinning weights in any shared page ([SMPL-X licence](https://smpl-x.is.tue.mpg.de/modellicense.html)). GVHMR's weights are described secondhand as non-commercial research only, GENMO is under an NVIDIA non-commercial licence, and CameraHMR is research-only, so any of them is acceptable as a private comparison and none as a shipped component. RacketVision's data is MIT ([dataset card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/zonetom67/RacketVision)). None of this is legal advice.

**The plan (judgement).** Each stage ends in something viewable, and the first is the gate for the rest.

| Stage | Work | Done when |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Benchmark | Run 3DB (MLX port and MPS fork) on all four players for one 30-second rally with your boxes and intrinsics; run GVHMR static mode as the control | You have seconds per person-frame on your Mac mini and a side-by-side view of far-player quality |
| 2. Temporal and grounding layer | Lock MHR shape and scale per player; replace 3DB's translation with the court-plane foot tracks; pin stance feet; handle airborne spans ballistically; smooth joint rotations with joint- and speed-dependent strength | Bodies move without jitter or foot slide over a full rally |
| 3. Paddle v1 | Rigid paddle model on the wrist with a per-player grip transform; face normal from ball velocity change at each hit; interpolate the grip-relative transform between hits | Every hit shows the paddle meeting the ball at a plausible angle |
| 4. Web export | Convert MHR to a skinned mesh plus per-frame joint transforms; sample per-part colours from video; build the page | One rally plays in a single HTML file |
| 5. Paddle v2 | Label a few hundred to a few thousand pickleball paddle frames; fine-tune a keypoint or segmentation model from RacketVision weights; add wrist-anchored silhouette fitting for near players | Near-side paddle angle on slow frames visibly matches video |
| 6. Optional | Hand decoder on near players; InstantHMR for speed; a marked-paddle or second-camera capture to validate face angle | Only if earlier stages leave time or doubt |

Two notes on the plan. For paddle masks, SAM 3 has a community MLX image port and accepts noun-phrase prompts, but no source tests it on 15 to 80 px blurred paddles, so a small trained segmenter on hand-centred crops, as LATTE-MV used for table-tennis rackets, is the proven fallback ([mlx-community/sam3-image](https://huggingface.co/mlx-community/sam3-image); [LATTE-MV](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.20936v2)). And any labelled evaluation set for the paddle model should be a random sample of frames, kept apart from hand-picked hard cases, because RacketVision's own numbers come from evenly sampled frames that under-represent the blurred contact window.

## The web page ships one rig per player and a few megabytes of rotations

A self-contained page has to carry the body model, so the choice of representation is forced by size and licence. Per-frame MHR vertices would cost about 2.65 GB per minute for four players; joint rotations cost about **10 MB per minute for four players at 50 fps** before base64's 33% overhead, and half that at 25 fps with interpolation in the viewer. The paddle adds about 84 kB per minute and the ball about 36 kB (the notes' arithmetic; MHR's joint count was not confirmed, so the body figure is approximate). The static mesh is paid once per player, on the order of 1 MB before quantisation.

The export needs custom work because no MHR-to-glTF tool was found ([MHR README](https://github.com/facebookresearch/MHR)). The notes' recipe, which is inference: run MHR forward kinematics offline and write per-joint local transforms per frame, since the 204 pose parameters are not joint quaternions; bake each player's identity into the rest mesh and skeleton once; prune and renormalise skinning weights to four influences per vertex, or skin in custom JavaScript as the `body-models-viser` plugin does to avoid that limit ([body-models-viser](https://pypi.org/project/body-models-viser/0.0.1/)); and drop the pose correctives at a small visual cost. In the page, load three.js from a CDN, decode the typed arrays into `SkinnedMesh` bones each frame, and treat paddle and ball as rigid objects with one transform per frame. A custom typed-array format is simpler than an animated GLB and needs no decompression library; a base64 GLB is the alternative if Blender interchange matters. Include the Apache-2.0 licence text for the rig.

For appearance, skip animatable Gaussian avatars. Published methods train 12 to 45 minutes per person on a CUDA GPU, none reports Apple Silicon results, and they assume a subject that fills the frame ([3DGS-Avatar](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.09228v3); [SAGA](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.00845v1)). The cheap route is to project each fitted mesh into a few frames and take median colours per body part (shirt, shorts, skin, shoes), stored as vertex colours. It makes the four players distinguishable for a few bytes per vertex, though no source documents it and it is the notes' own suggestion.

## Conclusion

The question "SAM 3D or something better" turns out to be the wrong axis. Per-frame pose accuracy is no longer the bottleneck for a court-anchored twin; the published models are within a few millimetres of each other on it, and all of them fail on depth, contact and shape, which the court supplies. What separates the options is whether the output can legally and practically live in a web page, and whether the model runs on the Mac with measured numbers. On both counts the MHR family stands alone, which makes the body-model licence, not the benchmark table, the deciding fact.

The paddle is where this project would be doing something new. No published system combines wrist kinematics, impact physics and image evidence for a racket, and consumer pickleball products expose no paddle orientation at all, so even a plausible paddle through a rally is ahead of what is public. It also means there is no accuracy reference: the 15 to 30 degree expectation is borrowed from table tennis, and only a small ground-truth capture will say whether pickleball's stiffer face and slower ball make it better or worse. The 30-second benchmark settles the two remaining unknowns that no source can, how 3DB looks on an 80 px player and what a minute of video costs on this machine, and both answers arrive in an afternoon.
